# Tearoom (Chaen)

**Saeki Shunkō (1909–1942)**  
1936 · Ink, color, and silver on paper  
264.2 × 198.1 cm · Museum of Fine Arts, Boston

This one has a special place on our company's gallery wall. *Tearoom* feels like a little window into Japan at a moment when everyday life was changing quickly. Two café waitresses stand side by side in matching red, blue, and yellow uniforms, surrounded by carefully arranged plants and modern fixtures. Their bobbed hairstyles and Western-style outfits sit alongside the familiar visual language of Japanese painting.

I particularly love how Shunkō brings traditional Japanese sensibilities and modern life together. The composition feels almost deliberately arranged: the geometric floor, the metal plant stand, the neat rows of pots, and those strong blocks of color. The pigments give the scene a distinct atmosphere for me — I get a real sense of Tokyo's urban life in the 1930s from it. It feels modern without losing its connection to the visual traditions that came before it.

I first came across Shunkō around three years ago, in a magazine feature about his life. That was when I first learned his name and started looking into his work. Finding *Tearoom* again, now hanging on our own gallery wall, makes the connection feel a little more personal. It's one of those paintings I can keep looking at and still find something to appreciate.

Shunkō was born Saeki Toshio and worked as a designer at Tokyo's Shirokiya Department Store before becoming known for paintings that brought traditional Japanese techniques into conversation with contemporary fashion, interiors, and city life. His career was tragically short; he died in 1942, aged 33. Even so, works like this give us a vivid glimpse of the changing world he lived in.
