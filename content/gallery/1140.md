# The Great Wave off Kanagawa

*An image that seems to follow you everywhere — and somehow never gets old.*
:::

---

## 01 — An Image I've Seen Everywhere

There are some works of art that you recognize before you even remember their title. **The Great Wave off Kanagawa** is definitely one of them.

I've seen this image almost everywhere: in shows, on social media, in anime, on posters, in little references and homages scattered across the internet. It's one of those artworks that has travelled so far beyond its original context that you can encounter it without even looking for it.

And honestly, I never get tired of seeing it.

The more I came across it, the more fond I became of the artwork itself. It's incredibly recognizable, of course, but I think there's a reason it has stayed with people for so long. There's so much going on in the composition, and yet it all comes together in a way that feels effortless.

## 02 — The Wave, the Boats, and Mount Fuji

Created by **Katsushika Hokusai**, the print is formally titled *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* (*Kanagawa-oki nami-ura*). It was made around **1830–1832**, during Japan's Edo period, and belongs to his celebrated series *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* (*Fugaku sanjūrokkei*).

The first thing that catches my eye is, naturally, the wave. It takes up so much of the composition, curling over the boats with those almost claw-like tips of foam. The water looks powerful and chaotic, but the individual lines and shapes are so carefully arranged. Even the smallest curls seem deliberate.

Then there's Mount Fuji, sitting quietly in the distance. It's easy to miss at first because the wave is so dominant, but that contrast is part of what makes the image work. The mountain looks almost tiny beside the enormous water in the foreground. The boats and the people in them make the scale feel even more dramatic.

I love that Hokusai manages to make the scene feel both enormous and carefully composed. The wave is almost overwhelming, but your eye keeps moving through the print: from the boats, to the foam, to the open space, and finally toward Fuji.

## 03 — Those Blues

The colours are a huge part of why I love this print.

That deep blue, the lighter blue running through the water, and the warm, aged-looking paper create such a beautiful contrast. The foam stands out sharply against the darker areas, giving the wave its movement and texture. The lines are just as important as the colours; they make the water feel alive without turning the whole image into visual noise.

Hokusai's use of **Prussian blue**, a relatively new imported pigment in Japan at the time, is especially notable. The Metropolitan Museum of Art discusses how this pigment helped shape the look of the series and how the printing process itself adds depth to the different areas of blue.

It's one of those details that makes me appreciate the print even more. The colour isn't simply there to make the image look striking. It is part of how the whole scene is built.

## 04 — An Icon That Keeps Reappearing

It's hard to talk about this work without mentioning just how far its influence has travelled. The Great Wave has become one of the most recognizable images associated with Japanese art, and its design has been reproduced, referenced, reinterpreted, and borrowed in countless ways.

That's probably why I've encountered it in so many different places. Sometimes it's presented as a direct reproduction; other times it's an illustration, a visual joke, a background detail, or an unmistakable reference in a completely different work.

I think there's something lovely about that. An artwork made as part of a series of landscape prints in nineteenth-century Japan has become an image that people across the world can recognize today. It keeps finding new audiences, even in places and formats Hokusai could never have imagined.

## 05 — A Special One for the Collection

This is one of those pieces I wanted to include simply because it means something to me as an admirer of Japanese art.

I love the colours, the linework, the composition, and the way every part of the image seems to have its own movement. It's iconic, yes, but it doesn't feel overfamiliar to me. Every time I look at it, I find myself appreciating another little detail.

There are plenty of famous artworks that I recognize, but this is one I've genuinely grown fond of. It has earned its place in the collection.

## Artwork Details

- **Title:** *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* (神奈川沖浪裏), commonly known as *The Great Wave*
- **Artist:** Katsushika Hokusai (葛飾北斎, 1760–1849)
- **Series:** *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* (*Fugaku sanjūrokkei*)
- **Date:** circa 1830–1832
- **Period:** Edo period (1615–1868)
- **Medium:** Polychrome woodblock print; ink and colour on paper
- **Dimensions:** 25.7 × 37.9 cm (Metropolitan Museum of Art impression)
- **Collection reference:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
- **Object number:** JP1847
